How it works

Maps

An iterated function system (IFS) is a handful of affine maps. Here each map is drawn as a rectangle: it takes the dashed frame and squeezes, turns and moves it onto that rectangle. The F inside shows the orientation. Drag a rectangle, pull its corners or turn it by the round handle, and the picture changes at once.

The chaos game

Start anywhere. Pick a map at random, with probability given by its weight p, and move the point to its image. Repeat, and plot every point. The first few points are thrown away (burn-in) because they are still on their way. The rest settle on the attractor: the one shape that the maps leave unchanged as a whole. Slow the speed to 3/s and press Clear to watch it happen point by point.

Levels

The other way to build the same shape: draw a starting shape, replace it by its images under every map, and repeat. Whatever you start with, the pictures converge to the same attractor. This is the Hutchinson operator, and the Levels method shows it.

Contraction

The maps must shrink distances, otherwise points fly away. The Analysis box checks every map and warns about the ones that stretch. A rectangle larger than the frame is such a map.

Weights

The weights only change how often each map is used. A tiny rectangle with a large weight is overexposed, a large one with a small weight is pale. By area gives an even density, and the fern is the classic example where uneven weights are what make it look right.

Dimension

If every map scales by ri and the copies hardly overlap, the dimension d solves Σ rid = 1. The gasket has three maps at ½, so 3·(½)d = 1 and d = log 3 / log 2 ≈ 1.585. For maps that stretch unevenly, the app uses the geometric mean of the stretches, which is only an estimate.

Collage challenge

Pick a target in the Target tab and cover its ghost with your rectangles. The score compares the two shapes on a coarse grid. This is how fractal compression was imagined to work: find maps whose collage reproduces the picture.
